## Scene Checklist

Before marking a scene as ready to draft, verify:

- [ ] Scene has a clear POV character with a goal
- [ ] There is meaningful conflict (not just activity)
- [ ] The outcome changes something (plot, character, or reader knowledge)
- [ ] The scene connects to at least one other scene (setup or payoff)
- [ ] The emotional shift is intentional and trackable
- [ ] The scene is necessary (can the story work without it? If yes, cut it)
- [ ] The chapter ending creates reader pull, not just completion
- [ ] If the scene depends on reality, the relevant facts are researched or flagged
- [ ] The scene has a runway target, and it is under 250 words
- [ ] The anchored beat positions are arithmetically possible inside the estimated word count
- [ ] The prohibitions are filled in, not left blank
- [ ] The closing move differs from the previous three scenes' closing moves
- [ ] The scene's opening move is not shared with any chapter opening in any other novel in `novels/` <!-- check the first 200 words of every existing chapter one. Two novels opening on "The [noun] was wrong." followed by a taxonomy of wrongness is one scene written twice. -->
- [ ] Somebody in this scene wants something the POV character cannot simply be given

## Opening Scene — additional requirements

The first scene of the novel carries the whole book's rejection risk, and it is where an unedited draft most reliably fails. Measured: chapter-one openings in an unedited corpus took 426-513 words to reach an intentional act, against 84-147 for published openings, and stated an explicit want in 1-2 cases out of 7 against 5 out of 6.

The opening scene must additionally satisfy:

- [ ] Somebody wants something on the page, and something is opposing it, inside the runway
- [ ] It is not an arrival, a tour, or an inventory of a space — unless something is already wanted while that happens
- [ ] It does not establish setting before the POV character acts
- [ ] It does not close by naming the protagonist's competence, theme, or wound
- [ ] The protagonist's specific limitation is *used* by the scene, not described by it
